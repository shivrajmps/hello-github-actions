|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 |   0.5s |  10.1s | P/E. Price to Earnings. The most used, and most misused, number in the stock market. Let's build it from the accounts, line by line. | Title 'P/E = Price ÷ Earnings', subtitle 'most used · most misused', ledger book |
| 2 |  10.6s |  26.0s | Start at the profit and loss statement. Revenue at the top, one thousand crore. Minus costs, eight hundred and twenty. Minus interest, thirty. Minus tax, fifty. | 3 |  26.5s |  33.6s | Divide that by the number of shares, ten crore shares, and you get earnings per share: ten rupees. That is the E. | Arrow to the right: ₹100 cr ÷ 10 cr shares = ₹10 per share; 'EPS → the E' ringed |
| 4 |  34.2s |  42.7s | The P is just today's share price. Say two hundred rupees. So P/E is two hundred divided by ten, which is twenty. | Price today = ₹200; P/E = 200 ÷ 10; '= 20×' ringed |
| 5 |  43.3s |  62.4s | Read it like the shop. At a P/E of twenty, you are paying twenty rupees for every one rupee of yearly profit. If profits stayed flat, it would take twenty years to earn your money back. Flip it over: one divided by twenty is a five percent earnings yield. Now compare that with a fixed deposit. | Board clears. 'What does a P/E of 20 mean?': coins ₹20 per ₹1; clock 20 years; highlighted earnings yield 1÷20 = 5%; FD comparison line |
| 6 |  62.9s |  82.2s | Three warnings. A high P/E is not always expensive: the market may expect fast growth. A low P/E is not always cheap: profits may be about to fall. 'Three warnings' with red triangles; highlighted 'Compare with: same industry + own history' |

## 03-pb-ratio  (92 s)

| # | In | Out | Narration (trigger) | On-screen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 |   0.5s |  13.8s | P/B. Price to Book. This one does not care about profit. It asks a simpler question: what is the company worth on paper, if you added up everything it owns and paid off everything it owes? | Title 'P/B = Price ÷ Book value', 'doesn't care about profit', magnifier, 'worth on paper = owns − owes' |
| 2 |  14.3s |  30.0s | Open the balance sheet. On one side, everything the company owns: cash, factories, inventory, money customers owe. Say five hundred crore of assets. On the other side, everything it owes: loans and unpaid bills. Say three hundred crore. | Board clears. Balance-sheet T: OWNS (cash, factories, inventory, customer dues) ₹500 cr | OWES (loans, unpaid bills) ₹300 cr |
| 3 |  30.6s |  41.1s | Assets minus liabilities is book value, also called shareholders' equity: two hundred crore. That is the accountant's answer to the question, what is left for the owners. | Highlighted 500 − 300 = ₹200 cr; 'Book value = shareholders' equity = what's left for the owners' |
| 4 |  41.7s |  51.5s | Divide by ten crore shares, and book value per share is twenty rupees. The share price is forty. So P/B is forty over twenty, which is two. | Board clears. ₹200 cr ÷ 10 cr shares = ₹20; Share price ₹40; P/B = 40 ÷ 20 '= 2×' ringed |
| 5 |  52.0s |  68.2s | A P/B of two means the market pays two rupees for every one rupee of net assets on the books. Below one, you are buying assets for less than their stated value. That is either a bargain, or the market thinks those assets are not really worth what the books say. | Board clears. Number line 0×–3× with marker at 2×; 'below 1×' highlight; 'a bargain… or the books are overstated?' |
| 6 |  68.8s |  90.3s | Where it shines: banks, insurers, and asset-heavy businesses, where the balance sheet is the business. Where it fails: software or brands, whose real value, people and ideas, never shows up as an asset. And always pair it with return on equity. A company earning twenty percent on its book deserves a higher P/B than one earning five. | Board clears. Works well (bank icon: banks, insurers, asset-heavy, ticks) vs Fails (laptop: software, brands, crosses); highlighted ROE pairing |

## 04-ev-ebitda  (98 s)

| # | In | Out | Narration (trigger) | On-screen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 |   0.5s |   9.1s | EV to EBITDA. Sounds scary. It is actually the fairest comparison tool we have. Think of buying a house that still has a loan on it. | Title 'EV / EBITDA', 'the fairest comparison tool', house sketch with red LOAN tag |
| 2 |   9.7s |  32.7s | The price you pay the owner is like market cap: share price times number of shares. Say one thousand crore. But you also inherit the loan: four hundred crore of debt. And you get the cash sitting in the company's bank: one hundred crore. So the real cost of the whole business is one thousand, plus four hundred, minus one hundred. Enterprise value: one thousand three hundred crore. | House stays. Right column: Market cap ₹1,000 cr; + Debt ₹400 cr; − Cash ₹100 cr; rule; highlighted Enterprise value = ₹1,300 cr + check |
| 3 |  33.3s |  53.0s | Now the earnings side. Start with operating profit from the P&L: one hundred and fifty crore. Add back depreciation and amortisation, the paper charges for wear and tear on machines: fifty crore. That gives EBITDA, earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: two hundred crore. | Board clears. Operating profit ₹150 cr; + D&A ₹50 cr (paper charge note); rule; highlighted EBITDA = ₹200 cr; acronym expansion |
| 4 |  53.6s |  65.3s | EV over EBITDA is one thousand three hundred divided by two hundred: six and a half times. The whole business, loans included, costs six and a half years of cash operating profit. | Board clears. EV ÷ EBITDA = 1,300 ÷ 200; '= 6.5×' ringed; clock '6.5 years of cash operating profit, loans included' |
| 5 |  65.9s |  82.6s | Why bother? Because P/E gets distorted by debt and by tax rates. Two identical factories, one funded by loans and one by equity, show very different net profits. EV to EBITDA strips that out and compares the engine, not the financing. | Board clears. 'Why bother?': two factories (loans vs equity), 'net profit: different' in red, highlighted 'EV/EBITDA: the same' |
| 6 |  83.1s |  96.2s | The catch: EBITDA ignores the money needed to replace those machines. A business that must keep spending heavily on equipment is worth less than its EBITDA suggests. So always glance at capital expenditure too. | Board clears. Red triangle 'The catch: EBITDA ignores the cost of replacing those machines'; highlighted 'Always glance at CAPEX too' |
